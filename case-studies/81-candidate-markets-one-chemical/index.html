The lead that did not survive

The top-scoring lead was rejected. Every document behind it turned out to describe a business the client already ran, under a name the board had not matched — not white space at all, but the same business under another name. The second lead was put through the same check and survived it.

The three questions, answered

Is it real?Yes, for the chemistry.

The family is already sold commercially into that application, which proves it rather than predicts it. Not yet proven for the client's own material — and the report says so rather than glossing it.

Is it worth it?Register-verified, not brochure-claimed.

US volumes for the molecule and its family have risen sharply since 2020 while imports of the family's trade basket have fallen — import substitution, confirmed in two official sources rather than claimed in a brochure.

Can we win?The file is clean; the field is not.

Clean regulatory standing on both sides of the Atlantic, and new EU registrants have appeared in recent years — others are moving. The incumbents and the patents sit elsewhere, so the window is measured in filings per year rather than in sentiment.

Why publish a lead that failed?
Because a ranked list nothing falls off is a list nobody checked. The rejection above is the evidence that the ranking is doing work: the score said best lead, the sources said otherwise, and the sources won.
Every search is kept, so the list can be audited rather than believed.
What did a week of PROWL actually produce here?
A sourced go-to-market foundation, two leads killed with the reasons on the record, and many more still to check. The killed leads are the evidence that the ranking is doing work — a list nothing falls off is a list nobody checked.
Volume and trade-basket movements are register-verified in two official sources. The figures themselves stay in the client's report.
